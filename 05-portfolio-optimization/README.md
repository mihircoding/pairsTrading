# Project 5 — Portfolio Optimization & Risk

Implement Markowitz mean-variance optimization from scratch — minimum-variance and
maximum-Sharpe portfolios, the efficient frontier — then confront the reason nobody uses
textbook Markowitz in production (estimation error) and implement the standard fixes:
covariance shrinkage and risk parity.

This project is the statistics counterweight to the engineering-heavy projects 2 and 4.
It's also the one where the *limitations* are the real lesson: the gap between "optimal
on paper" and "robust in practice" is most of what risk teams do all day.

## The theory in one page

An asset universe has expected returns `mu` (vector) and covariance `cov` (matrix).
A portfolio is a weight vector `w` summing to 1. Then:

```
expected return : w' mu
variance        : w' cov w
volatility      : sqrt(w' cov w)
Sharpe ratio    : (w' mu - rf) / vol        (rf = risk-free rate, often 0 here)
```

Markowitz (1952): for any target return there is a weight vector minimizing variance —
the set of those portfolios traces the **efficient frontier**, a curve in (vol, return)
space. Two portfolios matter most:

- the **global minimum-variance portfolio** — the frontier's leftmost point; notable
  because it needs *no* expected-return estimates at all
- the **max-Sharpe (tangency) portfolio** — the frontier point with the best
  return-per-unit-risk

With no constraints these have closed forms via `inv(cov)`. With real-world constraints
(no shorting, position caps) you need a numerical optimizer — that's what you'll build,
using `scipy.optimize.minimize` (SLSQP handles the sum-to-one equality constraint and
per-asset bounds cleanly).

## Why textbook Markowitz fails in practice

The optimizer is only as good as `mu` and `cov` — and those are *estimated* from noisy
history:

- Expected returns are almost unestimable: a stock with 20% annual vol needs ~100 years
  of data before your sample mean has a standard error under 2%. The optimizer takes
  those noisy `mu` estimates literally and piles into whatever was luckiest —
  Michaud called mean-variance optimizers "error maximizers".
- The sample covariance matrix is noisy too: with N assets and T days you're estimating
  N(N+1)/2 numbers. For N=50, T=500 that's 1,275 parameters from 25,000 observations —
  the extreme eigenvalues are badly biased, exactly the directions the optimizer exploits.

The standard responses, both implemented here:

- **Shrinkage** (Ledoit-Wolf): pull the sample covariance toward a structured target —
  `cov_shrunk = (1-a) * sample + a * target`. You implement the simple
  shrink-to-diagonal version; `sklearn.covariance.LedoitWolf` gives the optimal `a`
  to compare against.
- **Risk parity**: give up on `mu` entirely and allocate by risk. Inverse-volatility
  weighting is the crude version; **equal risk contribution** (each asset contributes
  the same share of portfolio variance) is the real one, and needs a small numerical
  solve of its own.

## Build it: milestones

**Milestone 1 — Estimates** (`src/returns.py`)
Daily simple returns from prices; annualize the mean (×252) and covariance (×252).
Easy warmup, but the tests pin the conventions so everything downstream agrees.

**Milestone 2 — Portfolio arithmetic + min-variance** (`src/optimizer.py`)
`portfolio_performance` (the three formulas above), then `min_variance_weights` with
scipy. The test checks the 2-asset case against the closed form
`w1 = (s2² - s12) / (s1² + s2² - 2*s12)` — if your optimizer can't match algebra,
the constraint wiring is wrong.

**Milestone 3 — Max Sharpe** (`src/optimizer.py`)
Same machinery, different objective (minimize negative Sharpe). Watch out: Sharpe is
not convex in `w`, but with long-only bounds and sensible starts SLSQP behaves. The
test builds a case where the answer is obvious (one asset dominates) and checks you
find it.

**Milestone 4 — The frontier** (`src/frontier.py`)
Sweep target returns between the assets' min and max `mu`; for each, minimize variance
subject to hitting the target. Collect (vol, ret, weights). Plot it in the driver —
every finance textbook figure, generated by your own code.

**Milestone 5 — Robustness** (`src/risk_parity.py`)
Shrinkage, inverse-vol weights, and equal-risk-contribution weights. The ERC test is
strict: every asset's risk contribution must come out equal. Then the driver's
comparison — min-var vs max-Sharpe vs ERC weights on the same data — shows you
*why* risk parity took over institutional allocation: look at the concentration.

## Pitfalls checklist

- Annualizing volatility with ×252 instead of ×√252 (variance scales with time,
  vol with its square root).
- Feeding the optimizer prices instead of returns, or mixing daily mu with annual cov.
- Letting weights go slightly negative through the "long-only" constraint because you
  passed bounds wrong — the tests check `w >= -1e-8`.
- Reading equal *risk contribution* as equal *volatility weighting* — they coincide only
  when correlations are all equal.
- Backtest the optimized weights out of sample (stretch goal) before believing any of it.

## Stretch goals

- Rolling out-of-sample horse race: re-estimate weights each quarter on trailing data,
  hold for the next quarter; compare equal-weight vs min-var vs max-Sharpe vs ERC.
  (Spoiler worth verifying: equal-weight is embarrassingly hard to beat — see DeMiguel
  et al., "1/N".)
- Add a maximum-weight constraint (e.g., 25% cap) and watch the frontier deform.
- Black-Litterman: blend market-implied returns with your own views; fixes the
  garbage-mu problem from the other direction.
- Compare your shrinkage to `sklearn.covariance.LedoitWolf` on real data — how close
  is your simple version?

## Resources

- Markowitz (1952), "Portfolio Selection", *Journal of Finance* — 15 readable pages
  that won a Nobel; read the original.
- Ledoit & Wolf, "Honey, I Shrunk the Sample Covariance Matrix" — the shrinkage paper;
  free preprint online, and unusually well written.
- DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009), "Optimal Versus Naive Diversification" — the 1/N
  paper; the essential dose of humility for this whole field.
- Roncalli, *Introduction to Risk Parity and Budgeting* — the risk-parity reference;
  a free summary paper version is on SSRN.
- PyPortfolioOpt documentation (https://pyportfolioopt.readthedocs.io) — a clean
  production-quality implementation of everything here; read its source *after*
  building your own, and its docs' "Cookbook" for practical gotchas.
- scipy.optimize.minimize docs — understand `SLSQP`, constraint dicts, and bounds;
  this API shows up everywhere in quant work.
